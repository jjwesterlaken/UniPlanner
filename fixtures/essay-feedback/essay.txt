The Enclosure Acts and the Making of the English Working Class

The enclosure of common land between 1750 and 1830 was the single most important cause of the industrial working class in England. Parliament passed over four thousand separate acts in this period, and the effect was to remove from ordinary country people the customary rights on which their households had depended for centuries. Without access to common pasture, fuel and gleaning, the smallholder had nothing left to sell but labour, and it was this dispossession above all that filled the new manufacturing towns.

The scale of the transformation was enormous. Something like six million acres passed from common use into private hands. Historians agree that this was the decisive break between the old rural order and modernity. The commissioners appointed to carry out each enclosure were drawn from the same landowning class that stood to benefit, and it is hardly surprising that the awards they made favoured those who already held title. Cottagers who could not produce documentary proof of a right they had exercised for generations simply lost it.

What made this process so effective was its legality. Enclosure was not theft in the ordinary sense; it was accomplished through the proper forms, by a Parliament that represented the interests of property. This is why the resistance it provoked took the shape it did. There were riots, certainly, and fences were thrown down in many parishes, but there was no general rising, because the thing being resisted wore the dress of law.

The connection to industry has sometimes been overstated. Recent work on migration patterns suggests that many of those who moved to the manufacturing districts came from areas where enclosure had happened long before, or had not happened at all, and that the pull of factory wages mattered at least as much as any rural push. Population growth alone would have produced a surplus of rural labour whether or not a single act had been passed.

Enclosure also changed the landscape itself, replacing the open fields and their scattered strips with the hedged rectangles that are now thought of as the traditional English countryside. The irony is considerable. What is remembered as timeless was in fact the product of a specific and contested legal process within living memory of the industrial revolution.

The dispossession of the commoners was, then, the mechanism by which an agricultural population became an industrial one. Deprived of the means of subsistence, they had no choice but to enter the factories. This is the central fact of the period and everything else follows from it.

A further point concerns the poor law. The Speenhamland system of subsidising wages out of the rates has been blamed for pauperising the agricultural labourer, and there is something in this, though the practice was never as widespread as its critics claimed and had largely disappeared by the 1830s.

It has been said that the English working class was present at its own making. The enclosures are where that making began.
